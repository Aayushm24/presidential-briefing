# AI agents replace entire workflows while most builders still treat them as features

[Ringg's AI agents](https://openai.com/index/ringg) resolve 65% of customer calls at 90% lower cost than GPT-4.1, while most companies are still testing chatbots.

The companies winning now, Ringg, Ema, Harvey, Meta, built agent-native products that replace entire workflow categories. They didn't add AI features to existing tools. They made the agent the interface. That architectural choice creates a cost structure advantage that traditional software companies can't match without rebuilding from scratch.

**Key takeaways:**
- Ringg achieves 65% call resolution across voice, chat, and WhatsApp using GPT-5.6 at 90% lower cost than GPT-4.1
- Meta's Connect keynote positioned Muse as their primary AI agent platform, extending to Ray-Ban glasses for embodied experiences
- Ema raised $140M total with 50+ business clients including Google and Microsoft for AI-native workflow automation
- Harvey uses GPT-6 Astra for structured legal drafting that directly displaces billable hours at law firms
- Garry Tan's strategic framework: "make agents want your product" versus "use agents to make people want your product"

### The 65% customer service breakthrough changes enterprise economics

[Ringg's customer service agents](https://openai.com/index/ringg) resolve 65% of inbound calls without human intervention. They handle voice, chat, WhatsApp, and web interactions in multiple languages using GPT-5.6. The cost reduction versus GPT-4.1 hits 90%.

These numbers matter because they're the first concrete proof that AI agents can replace human customer service at enterprise scale. Previous AI customer service solutions handled simple FAQs or routed calls. Ringg's agents complete the entire customer interaction from greeting to resolution.

The mechanism behind this breakthrough comes from GPT-5.6's improved reasoning capabilities combined with Ringg's agent architecture. The system maintains full conversation context across all channels. A customer can start on chat, switch to voice, then finish on WhatsApp. The agent remembers the full interaction history and customer preferences.

What changed to make this possible now? Three factors converged. First, GPT-5.6's reasoning improved enough to handle complex customer scenarios that required judgment calls. Second, the cost per token dropped far enough to make high-volume customer service economically viable. Third, Ringg built agent-native architecture instead of bolting AI onto existing call center software.

This creates immediate pressure on every enterprise with call centers. CFOs will benchmark their current cost per contact against Ringg's 90% reduction. Customer service leaders will measure their resolution rates against 65%. The companies that can't match these metrics face a choice: rebuild their entire customer service stack as agent-native or accept a permanent cost structure disadvantage.

The causal chain forward is predictable. Human customer service agents move to complex cases that require emotional intelligence or legal judgment. Routine support becomes fully automated. Call center real estate requirements shrink. The surviving customer service software vendors either rebuild as agent platforms or lose customers to companies like Ringg.

What I keep coming back to is the timing. Ringg achieved these results using GPT-5.6, which launched three months ago. The infrastructure for 90% cost reduction in customer service is available today through GPT-5.6, not in some future AI roadmap.

### Meta makes agents the primary interface across all products

[Meta's Connect keynote](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/23/everything-new-coming-to-metas-ai-agent-muse/) positioned Muse as the centerpiece of their AI strategy. CEO Mark Zuckerberg demonstrated Muse running on Ray-Ban glasses, handling complex queries through voice while users walk around.

This is platform-level thinking. Meta didn't announce new AI features for Instagram and WhatsApp. They announced that Muse becomes the primary interface across Meta's network. Users interact with Muse first, then Muse orchestrates the underlying apps.

The Ray-Ban integration creates embodied agent experiences. Users can ask Muse to identify objects, translate signs, or research topics they're looking at. The agent sees what the user sees through the glasses' cameras. It responds through bone conduction speakers that don't block ambient sound.

The technical breakthrough here is context persistence across devices. A conversation with Muse on Ray-Ban glasses continues smoothly when the user opens WhatsApp on their phone. The agent remembers what the user was looking at, what questions they asked, what tasks they started. That continuity creates user lock-in that individual AI features can't match.

Meta's bet assumes that users prefer talking to agents over tapping through app interfaces. Early evidence supports this assumption. Voice interaction reduces friction for complex tasks. Instead of opening apps, navigating menus, and typing queries, users speak their intent and Muse handles the execution.

The competitive implications ripple through every platform company. TikTok, Snapchat, and YouTube must match agent-native experiences or risk looking outdated. More importantly, developers now build for agent-first interfaces. Apps become services that agents call, not destinations that users visit directly.

I think Meta's timing is strategic. They're moving first on agent-as-platform while competitors focus on individual AI features. If Muse becomes users' primary interface for digital tasks, Meta controls the distribution layer that other companies depend on for customer reach.

### Enterprise workflow software gets eaten from below

[Ema raised $140 million](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/23/ema-raises-77m-as-ai-starts-eating-into-enterprise-software-and-services/) total funding and counts Google and Microsoft among its 50+ enterprise customers. The company builds AI agents that automate entire business workflows, not just parts of them.

[Harvey uses GPT-6 Astra](https://openai.com/index/harvey-from-context-to-confidence-with-astra) to produce structured legal documents. The system ingests case context, precedents, and client requirements, then generates drafts that lawyers review and finalize. This directly displaces billable hours that junior associates previously handled.

The pattern emerging across these companies is total workflow replacement, not workflow augmentation. Ema doesn't integrate with existing business process management software. It replaces it entirely. Harvey doesn't help lawyers write documents faster. It writes the first draft for them.

This architectural choice creates cost advantages that traditional software vendors can't match through incremental AI features. When Ema automates a complete workflow, the customer eliminates software licenses, user training, and process coordination overhead. When Harvey drafts legal documents autonomously, law firms reduce junior associate hours and increase partner usage.

Traditional enterprise software companies face a rebuild-or-die moment. Their products assume human users who need interfaces, dashboards, and approval workflows. Agent-native products assume AI users who need APIs, data access, and result validation. These are fundamentally different architectures.

The companies that try to add AI features to existing workflow software carry legacy technical debt. Their databases weren't designed for agent access patterns. Their APIs weren't built for high-frequency automated queries. Their security models assume human authentication, not agent-to-agent communication.

[Garry Tan's framework](https://x.com/garrytan/status/2102955139875397806) captures the strategic choice every startup faces: "make agents want your product" versus "use agents to make people want your product." The first approach builds for an AI-native world where agents are the primary users. The second approach uses AI as a marketing advantage for human-centric products.

The companies building for "make agents want your product" are creating the core infrastructure layer that other businesses will depend on. They're designing APIs that agents can call autonomously, data formats that agents can process efficiently, and pricing models that scale with agent usage patterns.

### Superforecasters missed AI progress badly, what that means for your roadmap

[Ethan Mollick shared data](https://x.com/emollick/status/2102926079791124760) showing how dramatically expert forecasters underestimated AI development speed. When asked in September 2025, superforecasters put the chance of AI resolving a Millennium Problem by September 2026 at 1.7%. Industry experts were more optimistic at 4.6%. Both groups also severely underestimated AI lab revenue growth.

These forecasting failures matter for builders and investors making multi-year plans. The people with the best track record for predicting complex events couldn't anticipate AI's actual trajectory. That suggests the standard planning horizons in tech, 18 months for product roadmaps, 3 years for market sizing, 5 years for venture fund deployment, assume predictability that doesn't exist in AI.

The specific numbers reveal the magnitude of miscalibration. A 1.7% probability for Millennium Problem resolution implies centuries-level difficulty. A 4.6% probability suggests decades-level difficulty. The actual pace of mathematical AI progress has been orders of magnitude faster than even optimistic expert predictions.

The revenue underestimation shows similar blindness to AI market velocity. Mollick doesn't specify the exact numbers, but if expert forecasters missed lab revenue badly in both directions, total market size and individual company scaling speed, then venture capital allocation models and competitive analysis frameworks are operating on outdated assumptions.

This forecasting failure suggests aggressive timelines for AI-native product development. If your roadmap assumes gradual AI capability improvement, you're probably planning too slowly. If your competitive advantages depend on AI remaining expensive or limited, those advantages will erode faster than expert predictions suggest.

The practical implication is building products that can scale with AI capability improvements rather than locking in current AI limitations. Design for agent-native workflows even if current AI can only handle parts of them. Price for automation even if current costs make full automation uneconomical. Hire for AI-first thinking even if current tools require significant human oversight.

The velocity mismatch also affects competitive analysis. When expert forecasters miss AI progress by orders of magnitude, traditional competitive intelligence frameworks become unreliable. Companies building AI-native products may achieve market advantages faster than incumbent analysis suggests. The window for defensive responses may be shorter than strategic planning cycles assume. This creates both significant opportunity and substantial competitive risk for every business sector across the global economy.

### The technical architecture shift behind agent-native products

The companies building agent-native products aren't just adding AI on top of existing software. They're redesigning their entire technical architecture around the assumption that AI agents, not humans, are the primary users.

This architectural difference creates competitive advantages that traditional software companies can't match through incremental AI features. When agents are the primary users, the product needs APIs instead of user interfaces, programmatic access instead of dashboards, and result validation instead of approval workflows.

Ringg's architecture exemplifies this shift. Their customer service agents don't simulate clicking buttons in a traditional call center interface. Instead, they directly access customer data through APIs, update records programmatically, and route complex cases to humans through structured handoff protocols.

The same pattern appears in Harvey's legal document generation. The AI doesn't interact with a word processor interface designed for human lawyers. It ingests structured case data, processes it through document templates, and outputs formatted legal documents that integrate directly into law firm workflows.

Ema's business workflow automation follows the same principle. Instead of building an interface where humans configure AI assistants, they built AI agents that directly orchestrate business processes through API calls to existing software systems.

This architectural choice has immediate cost implications. Agent-native products eliminate the overhead of user interface development, user training, and human workflow coordination. When the AI handles the entire process autonomously, customers reduce software licenses, support costs, and process management overhead.

For existing software companies, this creates a rebuild-or-die moment. Their databases weren't designed for high-frequency agent queries. Their APIs weren't built for autonomous access patterns. Their security models assume human authentication, not agent-to-agent communication.

The companies that try to add AI features to human-centric products carry this technical debt. They need to maintain two architectures: the original human interface and the new agent capabilities. This dual architecture is inherently more expensive and complex than agent-native designs.
