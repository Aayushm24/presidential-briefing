[Ryan Carson runs 15 concurrent Devin agents](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/i-spent-20000-on-devin-in-a-month) to handle engineering, customer success, and investor updates. All tracked with a handwritten list.

Agentic AI stopped being an experiment. Founders are spending serious money on production agent workloads right now. Most builders still think of AI agents as demos or side projects. Carson's $20,000 monthly Devin bill signals something different. General Intuition's [$6 billion valuation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/24/valor-point72-back-general-intuition-at-6b-valuation-as-ai-startup-pushes-into-robotics/) confirms it. Institutional capital is pricing in adoption, not potential.

- General Intuition raises at $6B pre-money for spatial AI agents. Institutional conviction that embodied AI is the next platform.

### The agent economics finally work at scale

Carson's operation proves the unit economics work when you commit to agent infrastructure. His setup runs customer success across multiple law firm accounts. It handles 40 daily pull requests with automated code review. It manages investor updates. All with agents that cost less than hiring equivalent human roles.

What makes Carson's approach work is systematic orchestration rather than ad-hoc automation. He manages 15 concurrent agents using folder organization and a physical paper list. No sophisticated dashboards or complex tooling. Each agent handles a specific domain with clear boundaries. His "Watchdog" agent replaces an entire customer success team by monitoring every law firm account for issues. It automatically escalates problems.

The key insight from Carson's experience: agents work best when they replace entire workflows, not individual tasks. His LAN PR agent doesn't just write code. It handles the complete pull request lifecycle from creation through merge. It closes the loop on 40 daily PRs without human QA review. This complete workflow ownership generates measurable ROI.

Carson moved almost entirely off local agents despite the cost savings. The reliability and capability gap between cloud models and local alternatives creates enough productivity difference to justify the expense. He still uses local models for one specific case: when he needs guaranteed response time for time-sensitive operations.

The orchestration model Carson uses reveals something deeper about how agent infrastructure actually scales. Most companies think about agents as individual tools that enhance human productivity. Carson thinks about agents as complete workflow replacements that reduce coordination overhead. The difference shows up in the unit economics.

When agents handle discrete tasks, they create coordination costs. Humans still need to manage handoffs between agent outputs and the next step in the process. When agents own complete workflows, they eliminate coordination costs. The human becomes the orchestrator, not the coordinator.

This shift explains why Carson's $20,000 monthly spend generates positive ROI while most agent experiments burn budget without measurable impact. The experiments optimize for task completion. Carson optimizes for workflow elimination.

The pattern extends beyond software engineering. Carson's customer success agent doesn't just monitor accounts. It manages the entire escalation process. When it detects an issue, it creates a ticket, assigns priority, and routes to the appropriate team member with context. The human receives a fully-formed problem with suggested solutions, not raw data requiring interpretation.

This workflow replacement approach requires a different mindset about agent design. Instead of asking "what tasks can this agent help with," Carson asks "what complete business process can this agent own." The difference between enhancement and replacement drives the unit economics.

The infrastructure requirements also change when agents own workflows instead of tasks. Task-oriented agents can run in isolation. Workflow-oriented agents need persistent state, shared context, and reliable execution guarantees. Carson's handwritten list isn't quaint, it's the simplest possible orchestration layer that ensures workflow agents don't collide or duplicate work.

The valuation multiple matters because it's institutional money, not venture capital betting on future potential. Point72 manages hedge fund assets that require measurable returns. When hedge funds invest $6 billion in spatial AI, they're modeling revenue streams from real agent deployments. Not hoping for technology breakthroughs.

These markets require agents to understand three-dimensional movement and physical constraints. The foundation model approach means they're building the infrastructure layer. Other companies will build spatial applications on top of it.

Spatial AI follows the same pattern: foundational infrastructure investment that enables agent applications.

The timing of this $6 billion bet coinciding with Carson's $20,000 monthly operational spend creates a clear pattern. Capital markets are pricing in agent adoption at both the infrastructure layer and the application layer simultaneously. The infrastructure bet (General Intuition) and the operational evidence (Carson) validate each other.

Institutional investors don't typically lead $6 billion rounds in pre-revenue companies unless they see clear evidence of market readiness. Carson's operational success provides that evidence. His willingness to spend $20,000 monthly on agent infrastructure demonstrates that the unit economics work for real business problems.

The spatial AI focus also reveals which direction institutional capital thinks agent development will take. Text-based agents solve existing workflow problems more efficiently. Spatial agents create entirely new categories of automation. The bigger valuation goes to the bigger market opportunity.

General Intuition's approach to building "generalized" spatial AI agents instead of task-specific robotics solutions suggests they're targeting the same workflow replacement model Carson uses. Instead of agents that help with specific physical tasks, they're building agents that can own complete physical processes.

The foundation model approach makes this workflow replacement strategy possible. ### Software companies face the AI harness mandate

Garry Tan's observation cuts to the strategic core: software companies must become AI harnesses for their customers or get replaced by competitors who do. The companies that survive will be the ones that integrate AI agents into their core value proposition, while companies that add AI features as afterthoughts will be replaced.

This creates a clear product design imperative. Every software company needs to answer: what would our product look like if AI agents could handle 80% of the work our customers do manually? The companies that build those agent harnesses own the customer relationship. The ones that don't become implementation details.

The "deterministic layers like APIs and SQL remain" part of Tan's prediction is equally important. Agents need reliable infrastructure to operate on. The companies that provide that infrastructure become more valuable as agent adoption accelerates. Not less valuable. Databases, APIs, monitoring tools, security layers all become more critical when agents replace human workflows.

Ethan Mollick's insight about frontier AI excelling at irregular, high-friction tasks reinforces this direction. Agents aren't replacing simple automation. They're handling the complex, draining work that humans avoid. Medical bill disputes, credit card chargebacks, complex form processing. These are exactly the tasks that create the most customer value when automated properly.

The harness model Tan describes requires a fundamental shift in how software companies think about their products. Most software today helps humans work faster. The AI harness model replaces the human work entirely. The software becomes an autonomous system that delivers outcomes, not a tool that enhances productivity.

This shift explains why Carson's agent approach works while most AI integrations fail to generate clear ROI. Carson built a harness. His agents don't help him code faster. They handle entire software engineering workflows without human involvement. The output is merged code, not suggestions for human developers to evaluate.

Most software companies implementing AI today are building productivity enhancements, not harnesses. They add AI-powered features that make existing workflows more efficient. But they don't eliminate the workflows. Users still need to manage the same coordination overhead, just with AI assistance.

The harness approach eliminates coordination overhead by owning complete workflows. Instead of helping users complete tasks faster, it completes tasks autonomously and reports results. This changes the relationship between user and software from operator to supervisor.

This supervisory model creates new product design challenges. Users need visibility into what the harness is doing without needing to manage each step. They need confidence that the harness will handle edge cases appropriately. They need the ability to intervene when the harness encounters situations outside its training.

Carson's handwritten list solves these challenges with the simplest possible interface. He can see what each agent is working on. He can pause or redirect agents when needed. He can add new workflows or modify existing ones without complex configuration. The interface matches the supervisory relationship.

The coordinated nature of this pricing strategy suggests OpenAI is moving beyond competing for model market share. They're competing for platform control. Luna pricing below DeepSeek eliminates the cost advantage of open alternatives. GPT-5.6 in Kiro eliminates the feature advantage of specialized coding assistants.

This platform play has implications for every company building on AI infrastructure. OpenAI isn't just offering cheaper compute. The timing of this price compression coincides with the evidence from Carson and General Intuition that agent infrastructure is ready for production deployment. OpenAI is positioning to capture the value created by companies proving that agent economics work.

### #3 Situational Awareness hedge fund collapse shows AI implementation risk

Situational Awareness, the AI hedge fund that went from "talk of Wall Street" to [SEC investigation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08-24/situational-awareness-star-ai-hedge-fund-that-nearly-imploded-now-being-probed-by-the-sec/) in record time, demonstrates the operational risks of deploying AI systems without proper oversight.

The fund's rapid rise and fall pattern reflects a common AI implementation failure mode: over-confidence in automated systems without sufficient human oversight. When AI systems work, they can generate impressive short-term results. When they fail, the failures compound quickly because the systems operate at machine speed and scale.

For builders creating AI-powered businesses, the Situational Awareness case study illustrates the importance of building monitoring and circuit breaker systems into any AI automation. The fund's near-implosion suggests their AI trading systems either made systematically poor decisions or operated outside intended parameters without adequate safeguards.

The SEC investigation adds regulatory complexity for any AI-powered financial services. Regulators are establishing precedents for how AI system failures translate to compliance violations. AI systems in regulated industries require documented decision-making processes and audit trails that can satisfy regulatory scrutiny.

This failure story provides a counterbalance to the success stories dominating AI coverage. While Carson's agent operation demonstrates the upside potential of AI automation, Situational Awareness shows what happens when AI systems operate without appropriate constraints and oversight mechanisms.

The contrast between Carson's success and Situational Awareness's failure illustrates the difference between controlled agent deployment and unconstrained AI automation. Carson maintains human oversight through his orchestration layer. His handwritten list ensures he knows what each agent is doing. Situational Awareness appears to have deployed AI systems with insufficient visibility into their decision-making processes.

The regulatory investigation also highlights the importance of building compliance into AI systems from the beginning, not adding it after deployment. In financial services, AI systems must maintain audit trails that document every decision. They must operate within pre-defined risk parameters. They must have human override capabilities for regulatory compliance.

These requirements create design constraints for AI systems in regulated industries. The systems must be transparent, predictable, and controllable. They must prioritize compliance over optimization. They must maintain human accountability even when operating autonomously.
